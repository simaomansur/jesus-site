Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy: Clinical Indications & Applications

Explore 20 conditions and clinical applications. Select any topic to read its explanation, the evidence behind HBOT, and selected clinical references—without leaving this page.

Established medical uses and emerging applications

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy has established uses for certain diagnoses, while other applications are still being studied. The FDA regulates and clears hyperbaric chambers as medical devices for specified uses. This distinction does not establish whether a particular patient qualifies for treatment or insurance coverage.

11 conditions

FDA-Cleared Clinical Uses

Conditions associated with established uses of FDA-cleared hyperbaric oxygen therapy devices. Many are also recognized by the Undersea & Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS).

Some uses listed here are medical emergencies and may require hospital-based care. Their inclusion is educational and does not mean RMHI offers emergency treatment. Coverage varies by diagnosis, payer, and individual circumstances.

Diabetic Foot Ulcers & Selected Non-Healing WoundsEstablished adjunctive use in selected nonhealing diabetic wounds.+

Understanding the Condition

Diabetic foot ulcers are difficult to heal because several problems often occur together: neuropathy hides injury, blood flow may be reduced, infection is common, and chronic hyperglycemia disrupts normal wound biology. When a wound stays hypoxic for too long, collagen production, angiogenesis, and immune function all suffer.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT can improve oxygen delivery to poorly healing tissue and support some of the core processes required for wound closure. Higher oxygen tension helps white blood cells fight infection, supports fibroblast function and collagen cross-linking, and can encourage the growth of new capillaries over time. These are all highly relevant in the diabetic foot.

What the Evidence Suggests

The best evidence supports HBOT as an adjunct for appropriately selected, refractory ulcers — usually after debridement, offloading, infection control, vascular assessment, and good local wound care are already in place. HBOT is not a substitute for those basics, but in the right wound it may improve healing trajectory and reduce amputation risk.

Clinical Takeaway

For diabetic foot ulcers, HBOT works best as part of a serious limb-preservation program, not as a stand-alone treatment.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Elraiyah T, et al. A systematic review and meta-analysis of adjunctive hyperbaric oxygen therapy in diabetic foot ulcers. 2016.
  2. Tao L, et al. Efficacy and safety of hyperbaric oxygen therapy in the management of diabetic foot ulcers. 2024.

Delayed Radiation Tissue InjuryEstablished for delayed radiation tissue injury in selected soft tissue and bony complications.+

Understanding the Condition

Radiation injury is really a wound-healing problem that may not show itself until long after cancer treatment ends. The affected tissue becomes fibrotic, hypovascular, and chronically oxygen deprived. That pattern can involve skin, muscle, bladder, rectum, mandible, chest wall, or other structures inside the radiation field.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT is useful because it directly addresses the hypoxic, poorly vascular environment left behind by radiation. Over a series of treatments, it can stimulate angiogenesis, improve fibroblast activity, and support the kind of tissue remodeling that irradiated tissue struggles to do on its own. This is why HBOT is often paired with surgery or advanced wound care in late radiation injury.

What the Evidence Suggests

The strongest role for HBOT is in delayed radiation injury, not as a routine treatment during active cancer therapy. Published reviews support its use across a range of soft-tissue and bony radionecrosis problems, especially when symptoms have become chronic and standard healing has failed.

Clinical Takeaway

When tissue has been permanently altered by prior radiation, HBOT is one of the few therapies that aims to rebuild the healing environment itself.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Feldmeier JJ, et al. A systematic review of the literature reporting the application of hyperbaric oxygen in delayed radiation injuries. 2002.
  2. Eckert KA, et al. Systematic Review of Hyperbaric Oxygen for Late Radiation Tissue Injury. 2025.

Radiation CystitisEstablished treatment option for selected chronic hemorrhagic or refractory radiation bladder injury.+

Understanding the Condition

Radiation cystitis happens when bladder tissue that was exposed during cancer treatment becomes chronically hypoxic, fragile, and prone to bleeding. Patients may develop urgency, frequency, pain, and hematuria months or even years after radiation. The bladder is not simply irritated; it has often undergone a long-term microvascular injury.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT is a strong fit for this problem because it can improve oxygen delivery to injured bladder tissue and stimulate the growth of healthier microvasculature over time. The aim is durable tissue repair, not just short-term symptom suppression. That is especially important in patients whose bleeding keeps recurring despite standard urologic care.

What the Evidence Suggests

Radiation cystitis is one of the best-supported delayed-radiation uses of HBOT. Clinical trials and longer follow-up studies suggest that many patients experience meaningful improvement in bleeding and bladder-related symptoms, sometimes after other treatment options have already failed.

Clinical Takeaway

For chronic radiation cystitis, HBOT is often considered when conservative measures are no longer enough and durable tissue healing is the goal.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Oscarsson N, et al. Radiation-induced cystitis treated with hyperbaric oxygen: a randomized controlled trial. 2019.
  2. Oscarsson N, et al. Radiation-induced cystitis treated with hyperbaric oxygen: 5-year follow-up. 2025.

Radiation ProctitisEstablished option for selected refractory late radiation rectal injury.+

Understanding the Condition

Radiation proctitis develops when rectal tissue exposed during pelvic radiation becomes inflamed, ischemic, and fragile. Patients may experience bleeding, urgency, pain, tenesmus, diarrhea, or poor bowel control. Like other delayed radiation injuries, the deeper issue is chronic microvascular damage rather than a simple temporary irritation.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT helps by increasing oxygen delivery to injured rectal tissue and encouraging the growth of more functional blood vessels over time. This can reduce bleeding, improve mucosal healing, and support recovery in tissue that otherwise remains trapped in a chronic low-oxygen state.

What the Evidence Suggests

This is one of the more studied pelvic radiation complications treated with HBOT. Randomized and observational data support improvement in selected patients with chronic, refractory symptoms, particularly when bleeding and tissue fragility continue despite medical or endoscopic management.

Clinical Takeaway

For radiation proctitis that has become chronic and difficult to control, HBOT is often considered as a tissue-healing strategy before moving toward more invasive escalation.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Clarke RE, et al. Hyperbaric oxygen treatment of chronic refractory radiation proctitis. 2008.
  2. Girnius S, et al. Treatment of refractory radiation-induced hemorrhagic proctitis with HBOT. 2006.

OsteoradionecrosisClassic delayed-radiation application, especially when healing is compromised around bone and surgery.+

Understanding the Condition

Osteoradionecrosis develops when irradiated bone — most often the jaw — becomes chronically hypoxic, fibrotic, and poorly vascularized. Patients may develop pain, exposed bone, infection, fracture risk, or poor healing after dental work. The problem is not simply infection; it is a long-term wound-healing failure in bone already injured by radiation.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT is used because it can improve oxygen tension in damaged tissues and stimulate angiogenesis in an area that has lost much of its normal vascular reserve. In practice, it is often used around debridement, extractions, or reconstructive procedures to support healing where ordinary tissue repair can no longer be assumed.

What the Evidence Suggests

The literature around osteoradionecrosis is complex, and results vary by stage of disease and surgical context. Even so, HBOT remains one of the best known delayed-radiation applications in hyperbaric medicine because the underlying biology fits so well with the treatment. It is most helpful when used thoughtfully within a broader oral surgery or head-and-neck plan.

Clinical Takeaway

For selected ORN cases, HBOT is best understood as a tissue-conditioning and healing-support tool, especially when surgery or exposed bone are part of the picture.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Marx RE, et al. A randomized prospective clinical trial of hyperbaric oxygen in the prevention of osteoradionecrosis. 1985.
  2. Forner LE, et al. Combined data from the two randomized clinical trials on hyperbaric oxygen treatment for mandibular osteoradionecrosis. 2022.

Compromised Skin Grafts & FlapsEstablished salvage adjunct for threatened, ischemic, or poorly perfused tissue transfer.+

Understanding the Condition

A healthy skin graft or flap does not usually need HBOT. The value of hyperbaric therapy appears when the reconstruction is in trouble — for example, when tissue is dusky, congested, ischemic, or healing over a compromised wound bed. In those situations, every hour of perfusion support can matter.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT increases oxygen diffusion into tissue that may not be getting enough oxygen through normal circulation. It can also reduce edema and help support neovascularization, fibroblast function, and collagen production. For reconstructive surgeons, that means HBOT may improve the odds that threatened tissue survives long enough to recover.

What the Evidence Suggests

The best use is early salvage of compromised tissue, not routine treatment of normal postoperative flaps. Published reviews and case series support its role when grafts or flaps show signs of failure, especially in irradiated or poorly vascularized wound beds. Timing and close surgical follow-up are critical.

Clinical Takeaway

HBOT is most useful when a graft or flap is at risk — not as a routine add-on when the reconstruction is already doing well.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Francis A, et al. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy for the Compromised Graft or Flap. 2017.
  2. Friedman HI, et al. An evidence-based appraisal of the use of hyperbaric oxygen on flaps and grafts. 2006.

Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss (SSNHL)Time-sensitive adjunct with meaningful clinical support, especially early after onset.+

Understanding the Condition

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is an ear emergency. Patients often wake up with muffled hearing, ringing, pressure, or a rapid drop in hearing in one ear. The inner ear has an unusually delicate blood supply, so even a short-lived disruption in oxygen delivery, inflammation, or microvascular flow can affect cochlear function.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT is used because it can sharply increase oxygen tension in inner-ear fluids and cochlear tissues during a period when those structures may be metabolically stressed. In practical terms, the goal is to help salvage hearing while the ear is still potentially recoverable, usually alongside corticosteroid treatment rather than instead of it.

What the Evidence Suggests

This is one of the better-supported nonwound uses of HBOT. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that HBOT, when added to standard medical treatment, can improve hearing outcomes in selected patients, particularly when treatment begins early and the hearing loss is severe. The benefit appears to be greatest when there is no long delay before therapy starts.

Clinical Takeaway

For sudden hearing loss, HBOT is best thought of as an early adjunctive treatment. The window matters, so patients usually do better with urgent evaluation than with a wait-and-see approach.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Joshua TG, et al. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy for Patients With Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss. 2022.
  2. Moghib K, et al. Evaluating hyperbaric oxygen therapy as an adjunct to corticosteroids in sudden sensorineural hearing loss. 2025.

Gas EmbolismDefinitive emergency recompression therapy in clinically significant cases.+

Emergency indication: This condition requires immediate medical evaluation. RMHI’s outpatient website is not an emergency service or a substitute for an emergency department. Call 911 when urgent symptoms are present.

Understanding the Condition

Gas embolism occurs when bubbles enter the circulation and block blood flow. When those bubbles travel to the brain, heart, or other critical organs, the result can look like a stroke, a heart injury, or sudden cardiopulmonary collapse. This is one of the clearest emergency uses of hyperbaric medicine.

How HBOT May Help

Pressure inside the chamber physically shrinks gas bubbles, which helps restore blood flow. At the same time, breathing 100% oxygen maximizes tissue oxygenation and speeds the replacement of inert gas within the bubble. The treatment is both mechanical and metabolic, which is why it is so effective when started promptly.

What the Evidence Suggests

For arterial or cerebral gas embolism, earlier HBOT is consistently associated with better outcomes. In practice, HBOT is used after line placement complications, diving injuries, surgery-related air embolism, and other situations where intravascular gas is causing neurologic or hemodynamic injury.

Clinical Takeaway

Clinically significant gas embolism is a true hyperbaric emergency. The priority is early recognition, oxygen administration, and urgent referral for recompression.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Moon RE, et al. Hyperbaric treatment of air or gas embolism. 2019.
  2. Fakkert RA, et al. Early hyperbaric oxygen therapy is associated with favorable outcome in patients with iatrogenic cerebral arterial gas embolism. 2023.

Carbon Monoxide PoisoningEstablished emergency use in selected poisoning cases.+

Emergency indication: This condition requires immediate medical evaluation. RMHI’s outpatient website is not an emergency service or a substitute for an emergency department. Call 911 when urgent symptoms are present.

Understanding the Condition

Carbon monoxide poisoning is dangerous because the gas binds hemoglobin tightly and interferes with oxygen delivery to the brain, heart, and other vital organs. Patients may present with headache, confusion, fainting, chest pain, neurologic symptoms, or exposure during a fire or equipment malfunction. The injury is not just low oxygen in the moment; delayed neurologic problems can develop later.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT addresses two urgent problems at once. First, it accelerates the removal of carbon monoxide from hemoglobin. Second, it floods plasma with dissolved oxygen so tissues can receive oxygen even while normal transport has been disrupted. That is why HBOT is considered in more serious poisonings, especially when there are neurologic symptoms, cardiac effects, pregnancy, or major exposure.

What the Evidence Suggests

Although trial results have not been perfectly uniform, HBOT remains a cornerstone consideration for severe or high-risk carbon monoxide poisoning because the physiologic rationale is strong and the stakes are high. In real practice, the question is often not whether CO is dangerous, but whether the patient meets criteria for urgent hyperbaric referral.

Clinical Takeaway

This is not a wellness use of HBOT. It is a time-sensitive emergency application designed to reduce acute injury and potentially limit delayed neurologic harm.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Buckley NA, et al. Hyperbaric oxygen for carbon monoxide poisoning. 2011.
  2. Weaver LK, et al. A randomized trial of one versus three hyperbaric oxygen treatments for acute carbon monoxide poisoning. 2023.

Gas GangreneEstablished emergency adjunct to surgery and antibiotics.+

Emergency indication: This condition requires immediate medical evaluation. RMHI’s outpatient website is not an emergency service or a substitute for an emergency department. Call 911 when urgent symptoms are present.

Understanding the Condition

Gas gangrene, or clostridial myonecrosis, is one of the fastest-moving soft-tissue infections in medicine. The bacteria thrive in low-oxygen tissue, release toxins, and can destroy muscle with shocking speed. Without prompt treatment, patients can deteriorate toward shock, limb loss, or death.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT is useful here because it changes the tissue environment in a way the organism does not tolerate well. High oxygen tensions inhibit anaerobic growth, help reduce toxin production, and support host defenses in tissue that is otherwise being overwhelmed. Importantly, this happens while the patient is also receiving surgery and antibiotics.

What the Evidence Suggests

HBOT has long been used as an adjunct in gas gangrene, but it is never the primary therapy. The life-saving steps remain aggressive surgical debridement, broad-spectrum antibiotics, ICU-level monitoring when needed, and repeated reassessment. Hyperbaric treatment supports those efforts by improving the oxygen environment in threatened tissue.

Clinical Takeaway

In gas gangrene, HBOT is a powerful adjunct — but only when paired with immediate definitive surgical and antibiotic management.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Hart GB, et al. Gas gangrene. 1983.
  2. Stephens MB. Gas gangrene: potential for hyperbaric oxygen therapy. 1996.

Crush Injuries & Acute Peripheral IschemiaEstablished adjunctive emergency use for threatened tissue and limb salvage.+

Emergency indication: This condition requires immediate medical evaluation. RMHI’s outpatient website is not an emergency service or a substitute for an emergency department. Call 911 when urgent symptoms are present.

Understanding the Condition

In crush injury and acute traumatic peripheral ischemia, tissue may be structurally damaged and oxygen-starved at the same time. Swelling increases pressure, blood flow falls, and a vicious cycle begins: more edema leads to less perfusion, which leads to more tissue loss. This is exactly the kind of environment in which HBOT can make physiologic sense.

How HBOT May Help

Even when circulation is compromised, hyperbaric oxygen can deliver unusually high amounts of oxygen through plasma diffusion, helping threatened tissue survive while definitive trauma care proceeds. HBOT also causes vasoconstriction without reducing oxygen delivery, which can help shrink edema and reduce compartment pressure. In practice, this can buy time for tissue at risk.

What the Evidence Suggests

This is a classic adjunctive hyperbaric indication. The key word is adjunctive. HBOT is used together with urgent trauma evaluation, vascular management, fasciotomy when needed, debridement, infection control, and orthopedic care. The best outcomes are usually seen when therapy is started early rather than after tissue loss is already irreversible.

Clinical Takeaway

For severe crush injury or acute ischemia, HBOT is best understood as limb- and tissue-salvage support while the rest of trauma management is underway.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Myers RA, et al. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy for trauma: crush injury, compartment syndrome, and other acute traumatic peripheral ischemias. 2000.
  2. Strauss MB. The role of hyperbaric oxygen for acute traumatic ischemias. 2022.

Additional / Non–FDA-Cleared Applications

These conditions are being researched or considered in selected clinical settings but are not established FDA-cleared indications for HBOT. The evidence ranges from early-stage to limited or inconsistent.

HBOT for these applications is investigational or off-label; it is not a substitute for established treatment and generally should not be assumed to be insured.

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)Emerging and still debated; strongest interest is in selected persistent symptom populations.+

Understanding the Condition

After traumatic brain injury, the problem is not only the initial impact. Many patients go on to develop a second phase of injury marked by altered blood flow, inflammation, metabolic mismatch, headache, fatigue, sleep disruption, and cognitive symptoms. This is why some people continue to feel unwell long after the original trauma is over.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT is being studied as a way to improve oxygen availability to vulnerable brain tissue, support mitochondrial function, reduce neuroinflammatory stress, and potentially encourage neuroplasticity. The brain is metabolically demanding, so even modest improvements in perfusion and oxygen delivery may matter in carefully selected patients.

What the Evidence Suggests

The literature is mixed. Some studies report meaningful gains in symptom burden, cognition, and quality of life, while others show smaller or less certain effects, partly because trial design, pressure protocols, timing, and sham comparisons vary so much. At this point, HBOT is not a universally accepted standard treatment for all TBI, but it remains an active area of clinical and research interest.

Clinical Takeaway

HBOT may be worth discussing in chronic or persistent post-concussive symptoms, but expectations should be thoughtful and individualized rather than oversold.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Crawford C, et al. Is Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Effective for Traumatic Brain Injury? 2017.
  2. Harch PG, et al. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Efficacy in Mild Traumatic Brain Injury and Persistent Postconcussion Syndrome. 2022.

PTSDEmerging neurologic-psychiatric application, especially in trauma-exposed populations with persistent symptoms.+

Understanding the Condition

PTSD is often discussed as a mental health diagnosis, but for many patients it is also a disorder of sleep, arousal, memory processing, autonomic dysregulation, and brain-network function. That is one reason HBOT has entered the conversation, particularly in military and blast-exposed populations where PTSD and mild brain injury often overlap.

How HBOT May Help

The proposed benefit is not that oxygen treats trauma in a simplistic sense. Rather, HBOT may support brain perfusion, neuroplasticity, and the function of networks involved in emotional regulation, cognition, and symptom reprocessing. In the right patient, that could translate into gains in sleep, mood, reactivity, cognition, and daily function.

What the Evidence Suggests

Early controlled studies, including work in combat-associated PTSD, are encouraging. However, this is not yet standard psychiatric care, and the field still needs clearer replication, protocol refinement, and better identification of which patients benefit most. HBOT should therefore be described as an adjunctive neurologic rehabilitation strategy, not a replacement for trauma-informed therapy.

Clinical Takeaway

PTSD is a promising area for HBOT research, but the responsible message today is cautious optimism rather than certainty.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Andrews SR, et al. Systematic review and dosage analysis: hyperbaric oxygen therapy for post-traumatic stress disorder. 2024.
  2. Doenyas-Barak K, et al. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy for Veterans With Combat-Associated PTSD. 2024.

Long COVIDPromising but still evolving; not yet a settled standard of care.+

Understanding the Condition

Long COVID often brings together fatigue, brain fog, poor exercise tolerance, sleep disruption, headache, mood changes, and dysautonomia. One of the reasons HBOT drew attention so quickly is that many Long COVID theories involve microvascular dysfunction, chronic inflammation, abnormal oxygen utilization, and persistent neurologic symptoms — all areas where hyperbaric physiology may matter.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT may help by improving oxygen delivery, supporting neuroplasticity, and influencing inflammatory and endothelial pathways. That combination is especially relevant in patients whose symptoms look neurocognitive, fatigue-based, or perfusion-related rather than purely structural. The goal is not to “cure” Long COVID in one step, but to help the body shift out of a stuck recovery pattern.

What the Evidence Suggests

Early trials and reviews are encouraging, with reports of improvement in cognition, fatigue, sleep, and quality of life in some patients. At the same time, not every study has shown clear superiority over sham treatment, so the field is still sorting out who benefits most, which protocol matters, and how durable the gains are.

Clinical Takeaway

Long COVID is one of the most interesting emerging areas for HBOT, but it should still be discussed as promising rather than proven.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Wu BQ, et al. Effects of Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy on Long COVID. 2024.
  2. Kjellberg A, et al. Ten sessions of hyperbaric oxygen versus sham treatment in long COVID. 2025.

Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS)Limited but interesting; still far from established standard care.+

Understanding the Condition

Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome is far more than ordinary tiredness. Patients commonly describe severe fatigue, brain fog, unrefreshing sleep, exertional crashes, and striking reductions in daily function. Because the condition is biologically complex and likely heterogeneous, no single therapy has emerged as a definitive answer.

How HBOT May Help

HBOT is being explored because some patients appear to have abnormalities involving brain perfusion, autonomic regulation, inflammation, or energy metabolism. By increasing dissolved oxygen and potentially affecting neuroplasticity and inflammatory signaling, HBOT may help a subset of patients whose symptoms are driven in part by impaired physiologic recovery rather than simple deconditioning.

What the Evidence Suggests

The evidence is still small. Early clinical studies and imaging reports suggest that some patients improve in fatigue severity, quality of life, or brain-metabolic measures, but the field remains preliminary and protocols are not standardized. For that reason, HBOT should be presented as an emerging option rather than a proven mainstream treatment.

Clinical Takeaway

For ME/CFS, HBOT may be worth considering in selected cases, but it should be framed honestly as a developing therapy with limited high-quality evidence.

  1. Akarsu S, et al. The efficacy of hyperbaric oxygen therapy in chronic fatigue syndrome. 2013.

Lyme DiseaseNot established; should be discussed cautiously and without overstating the evidence.+

Understanding the Condition

Patients with Lyme disease or persistent post-treatment symptoms sometimes seek HBOT because they continue to struggle with fatigue, pain, cognitive complaints, or slowed recovery despite having completed standard medical care. Those symptoms are real, but they do not automatically prove ongoing active infection, which is an important distinction.

How HBOT May Help

The rationale for HBOT in this setting is mostly theoretical and supportive: improved tissue oxygenation, modulation of inflammation, and assistance with recovery in patients who feel chronically unwell. What HBOT has not yet demonstrated is that it is a proven treatment for active Borrelia infection or a replacement for evidence-based antibiotic management when infection is present.

What the Evidence Suggests

The literature for Lyme-specific HBOT is sparse and much weaker than for classic hyperbaric indications. Much of the discussion comes from case reports, anecdotal clinical experience, or nonstandard treatment frameworks. That means HBOT should be described honestly as an experimental adjunct in this area rather than a validated core therapy.

Clinical Takeaway

For Lyme-related symptoms, HBOT may be considered as supportive care in select cases, but claims should remain modest and medically careful.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Huang CY, et al. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy as an effective adjunctive treatment for chronic Lyme disease. 2014.
  2. Taylor RS. Review of treatment options for Lyme borreliosis. 2005.

Autoimmune ConditionsInvestigational; best discussed as supportive care in selected complications, not as a universal autoimmune treatment.+

Understanding the Condition

Autoimmune disease is not one condition. It includes a wide range of illnesses in which the immune system misidentifies the body’s own tissue as a target. Because the biology differs so much from one diagnosis to another, any discussion of HBOT has to be careful and specific rather than broad and promotional.

How HBOT May Help

The main interest in HBOT comes from its anti-inflammatory and tissue-healing effects. Experimental work suggests it may influence cytokine signaling, oxidative stress responses, and immune-cell behavior. In real-world practice, the most plausible role is often supportive — for example, helping a poorly healing ulcer, ischemic tissue, or an inflammatory wound problem in a patient who also has autoimmune disease.

What the Evidence Suggests

The evidence is not strong enough to claim HBOT as a disease-modifying treatment for autoimmune illness in general. There are encouraging preclinical findings and some focused clinical reports, but the literature is still too uneven to present it as a replacement for rheumatology, neurology, gastroenterology, or immunology care.

Clinical Takeaway

HBOT may have value in selected inflammatory or wound-healing scenarios, but autoimmune disease as a category remains an area of research rather than standard hyperbaric practice.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Fang J, et al. Clinical efficacy and mechanisms of hyperbaric oxygen in rheumatic immune diseases. 2025.
  2. Chiou HYC, et al. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Alleviates the Autoimmune Response in Experimental Autoimmune Encephalomyelitis. 2021.

Sports Injury RecoveryEmerging adjunctive use; not a standard sports recovery treatment.+

Understanding the Condition

Athletes usually ask about HBOT when they are dealing with a muscle tear, tendon irritation, bone stress injury, or a stubborn soft-tissue problem that is not settling down as expected. The appeal is straightforward: injured tissue often swells, local blood flow becomes less efficient, and the body has to rebuild collagen, capillaries, and muscle fibers before strength fully returns.

How HBOT May Help

Inside a hyperbaric chamber, oxygen dissolves into plasma at far higher levels than normal. In theory, that can improve oxygen delivery to bruised or swollen tissue, reduce edema, and support fibroblast activity, collagen formation, and the early phases of tissue repair. That physiologic rationale makes sense, especially in tissues with borderline perfusion after injury.

What the Evidence Suggests

The research base is promising but inconsistent. Some studies suggest faster recovery in certain musculoskeletal injuries or exercise-induced muscle damage, while others do not show a dramatic performance advantage. For the average injured athlete, HBOT should be viewed as a possible adjunct rather than a shortcut around imaging, graded rehabilitation, load management, and return-to-play decision making.

Clinical Takeaway

HBOT may be reasonable for selected soft-tissue injuries when the goal is to support healing and reduce recovery burden, but it should complement — not replace — a good sports medicine plan.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Moghadam N, et al. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy in Sports Musculoskeletal Injuries. 2020.
  2. Johnson-Arbor K, et al. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy for high performance athletes. 2025.

FertilityInvestigational reproductive application; not established standard fertility care.+

Understanding the Condition

HBOT is sometimes discussed in fertility medicine because ovarian tissue, the endometrium, and sperm production all depend on healthy blood flow, oxygen delivery, and cellular repair. That has led researchers to ask whether carefully dosed hyperbaric exposure could improve the environment in which eggs, embryos, or sperm mature.

How HBOT May Help

The theoretical benefits include better tissue oxygenation, improved local vascular signaling, and support for healing in reproductive tissues under stress. Some studies have explored HBOT in poor ovarian response, thin endometrium, and male-factor infertility. The idea is not that oxygen alone creates fertility, but that it may improve the biologic conditions around assisted reproduction in selected patients.

What the Evidence Suggests

This area is still early. There are encouraging reports, but the evidence is not yet strong enough to present HBOT as routine fertility treatment. Outcomes in reproductive medicine are influenced by age, egg quality, sperm health, uterine factors, genetics, and protocol design, so HBOT should be framed as an adjunct under investigation rather than a proven answer.

Clinical Takeaway

HBOT may become a supportive option in carefully selected fertility cases, but it should never replace full evaluation by a reproductive specialist.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Meng W, et al. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy improves oocyte yield and embryo quality in poor ovarian responders. 2025.
  2. Kangal KO, et al. Assisted reproductive treatments with hyperbaric oxygen in male infertility cases. 2021.

Anti-Aging & LongevityExperimental wellness application; not an established medical indication for lifespan extension.+

Understanding the Condition

Interest in HBOT for healthy aging comes from a simple question: can intermittent exposure to very high oxygen levels trigger repair pathways that improve vascular health, skin quality, recovery capacity, or markers of biologic aging? That is a much more ambitious claim than treating a wound or a radiation injury, so it deserves extra caution.

How HBOT May Help

Researchers have proposed that carefully dosed hyperoxic exposure may stimulate adaptive responses involving angiogenesis, mitochondrial signaling, and cellular stress-response pathways. Some human work has also looked at blood-cell markers related to senescence and telomere biology, which explains why HBOT is now discussed in longevity circles.

What the Evidence Suggests

The early studies are intriguing, but they should not be confused with proof that HBOT slows aging in a broad clinical sense. Improvements in biomarkers, skin-related outcomes, or physical performance in selected groups are not the same as demonstrated prevention of disease or extension of lifespan. For now, anti-aging remains an investigational framing rather than a settled therapeutic use.

Clinical Takeaway

HBOT may eventually earn a role in healthy-aging medicine, but current evidence supports curiosity more than certainty.

Selected Clinical References

  1. Hachmo Y, et al. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy increases telomere length and decreases immunosenescence in isolated blood cells. 2020.
  2. Hachmo Y, et al. The effect of hyperbaric oxygen therapy on the skin of healthy aging adults. 2021.
